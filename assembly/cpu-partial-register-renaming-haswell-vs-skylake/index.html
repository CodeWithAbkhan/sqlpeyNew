“CPU Partial Register Renaming: Haswell vs. Skylake and Beyond”

How do modern CPUs, specifically Intel’s Haswell and Skylake architectures, handle operations on partial registers (like AL, AH) and how does this differ from earlier designs such as Sandy Bridge and Ivy Bridge? What are the implications for performance, and what are the practical ways to leverage or avoid these behaviors in assembly code?

The core of the issue lies in how CPUs manage register renaming for partial register accesses. Unlike older designs where lower byte registers (e.g., AL) might be renamed independently of their wider counterparts (e.g., RAX), newer architectures like Haswell and Skylake often merge these operations.

Key Behaviors Observed on Haswell and Skylake:

  • AL/RAX Renaming: AL is typically not renamed separately from RAX. If the high-byte registers (AH, BH, CH, DH) are untouched, the behavior mimics CPUs without separate partial-register renaming.
  • Write-Only Access: A write to AL merges into RAX, creating a dependency on RAX. Loads into AL, specifically micro-fused ALU+load uops, execute on powerful execution ports, suggesting a true merge on every write.
  • Sandy Bridge Behavior: Intel’s optimization manual indicates that Sandy Bridge may require a merging uop for AL, implying separate renaming. The manual states that after a write to AH, BH, CH, or DH followed by a read of a larger form of the same register, a merge micro-op is inserted, consuming an allocation cycle. Similarly, after a micro-op with a 1 or 2-byte destination register (not a source in the instruction) and before a read of its larger form, a merge micro-op is part of the flow. This suggests that an instruction like add al, bl might RMW the entire RAX on Sandy Bridge instead of renaming separately, especially if one of the source registers is part of RAX.
  • High-8 Registers: High-8 registers can be renamed independently. Write-only access to AH (e.g., mov ah, reg8 or mov ah, [mem8]) renames AH without a dependency on its old value, unlike mov-immediate. However, a read of AH when it’s dirty requires a merging uop.
  • Dependency Breaking: Writing to EAX/RAX (e.g., lea eax, [rsi + rcx] or xor eax, eax) clears the AH-dirty state, avoiding a merging uop. Writing to AX (e.g., mov ax, 1) triggers a merge of AH first, behaving like any other RMW of AX/RAX.
  • movzx Behavior:
    • movzx ebx, al has zero latency on Haswell and Skylake, indicating rename elimination. It triggers AH merging if AH is dirty. This suggests a connection between the dropping of low-8 renaming and the introduction of mov-elimination by Intel.
    • movzx eax, al is not eliminated at rename. mov-elimination on Intel CPUs does not work for same-register operations like mov rax, rax. It’s recommended to prefer moving between separate architectural registers with movzx for zero-extension.
    • movzx eax, bx is not eliminated at rename on Haswell or Skylake. It has 1c latency and uses an ALU uop. Intel’s manual only specifies zero-latency for 8-bit movzx.

Performance Implications:

  • Merging UOPs: Reading AX, EAX, or RAX triggers a merge uop, consuming front-end issue/rename bandwidth. The Register Allocation Table (RAT) tracks the high-8-dirty state. Even after AH retires, its data resides in a separate physical register from RAX, requiring an extra merging uop.
  • Read-Modify-Write (RMW): RMW operations on AL (e.g., inc al) merge without additional cost, within the ALU uop. This occurs without triggering a merging uop even if AH is dirty.
  • setcc ah: This instruction depends on the old AH value but still dirties it. It is likely implemented as a read-modify-write. Compilers rarely generate setcc ah, but it can occur in specific optimization scenarios.
  • LSD (Loop Stream Decoder): Making AH dirty can prevent a loop from running from the LSD, even without explicit merging uops. This appears to be a Haswell/Skylake behavior. Some sources suggest Sandy Bridge’s LSD might behave differently.
  • Extra Latency: Reading AH when it’s not dirty adds an extra cycle of latency for both operands. For instance, add bl, ah can introduce latency even if RAX and AH are not directly involved. This is observed even when unpacking bytes using movzx ecx, al / movzx edx, ah. However, reading AH when it is dirty does not add latency.

Observed Behaviors and Potential Quirks:

  • Sandy Bridge and Ivy Bridge: Evidence suggests Ivy Bridge might still rename low-16 and low-8 registers separately from the full register, similar to Sandy Bridge but unlike Haswell and later. Tests using movsx r16, r8 show varying throughputs across architectures, with Haswell and later exhibiting 1c throughput in certain test configurations, possibly due to writing to the same architectural register repeatedly.
  • movzx r16, r8 on Haswell/Skylake: Some measurements show a mysterious 0.58c throughput for movsx r16, r8 on Haswell. Newer versions of profiling tools might report sub-1-cycle latency for movsx r1_16, r2_8 on Skylake, but this appears to be measuring the latency of a false dependency rather than the instruction’s inherent latency.
  • AMD CPUs: AMD CPUs, known for not performing partial-register renaming, do not exhibit the same 1c throughput effect in these tests, suggesting a difference in how these operations are handled.
  • Microcode Updates: Updates to microcode on Skylake (e.g., revision 0xf0) can permanently disable the LSD due to corner-case correctness issues with AH-merging. This can impact loop performance.
  • mov ah, cl Behavior: On Skylake with updated microcode, times N mov ah, cl loops show a direct correlation between the number of mov ah, cl repetitions and the per-iteration cycle count, suggesting front-end throughput limitations, possibly due to a merging uop needing its own cycle.

Practical Code Examples:

Consider optimizing code for performance. Understanding these micro-architectural details can help avoid bottlenecks.

Example 1: Avoiding Partial Register Penalties

When expanding bytes, prefer using separate registers where possible to avoid merging uops.

; Less optimal: movzx eax, al ; May cause merge if AH is dirty and not cleared
; Potentially better:
    movzx ecx, al      ; Read AL, no merge for AH
    movzx edx, ah      ; Read AH, separate operation
    ; Use ecx and edx for further processing

Example 2: Clearing High-Byte State

If you need to modify a high-byte register and then use the full register, ensure the dirty state is managed.

; Example: Modifying AH and then using RAX
    mov ah, 0x55       ; Write to AH, AH becomes dirty
    ; ... other operations ...
    mov eax, 0         ; Write to EAX, clears AH-dirty state
    ; Now reading RAX or EAX will not incur a merge penalty due to AH

Example 3: Loop Optimization Consideration

If a loop involves modifying high-byte registers, be aware of potential LSD disablement or increased cycle counts.

; Loop involving AH modification
.loop:
    mov ah, cl        ; Modifies AH
    mov ax, bx        ; Merges AH into RAX
    dec ebp
    jnz .loop
; The above might be slower than expected due to AH handling.

Alternative Approaches and Considerations:

  • Compiler Intrinsics: For higher-level languages like C++ or C#, using compiler intrinsics or specific library functions might abstract away some of these low-level details, but it’s still beneficial to understand the underlying behavior for critical performance paths.
  • Profiling Tools: Tools like perf (Linux), VTune (Intel), or Evenement Tracing for Windows (ETW) are invaluable for observing the actual micro-op behavior and identifying bottlenecks related to partial register usage.
  • Agner Fog’s Microarchitecture Guides: These resources provide deep dives into CPU instruction timings and behaviors, serving as excellent references for such low-level optimizations.

The interaction of partial registers with the CPU’s renaming and execution units is complex and has evolved significantly across Intel’s microarchitectures. While newer CPUs like Haswell and Skylake have streamlined some of these operations, careful coding practices can still yield performance benefits by avoiding unexpected merge uops or latencies.

FAQs on CPU Partial Register Renaming

Q: What is partial register renaming in CPUs

ANS: Partial register renaming is a CPU technique where the smaller parts of a larger register (e.g., AL within RAX) can be treated as separate entities by the CPU’s renaming mechanism. This allows operations on the smaller parts to occur without necessarily invalidating or depending on the entire larger register.

Q: How does Haswell differ from Skylake in partial register handling

ANS: Both Haswell and Skylake largely merged AL operations with RAX, meaning AL isn’t typically renamed independently. They also generally exhibit similar behaviors regarding high-byte registers like AH. Differences might be subtle and relate to specific instruction timings or front-end throughput.

Q: Does Sandy Bridge rename AL separately from RAX

ANS: Intel’s optimization manuals suggest that Sandy Bridge might require a merging uop for AL, implying it could rename AL separately from RAX, unlike later architectures.

Q: What is a merging uop

ANS: A merging uop is a micro-operation that the CPU generates to combine a partial register write with the existing value of the full register. This is necessary when the CPU’s renaming mechanism treats partial registers as distinct entities or when a partial register write needs to be reconciled with the full register’s state before a subsequent read of the full register.

Q: Can accessing AH cause performance issues

ANS: Yes, accessing AH can introduce performance considerations. If AH is “dirty” (modified) and then the full RAX, EAX, or AX register is read, a merging uop is generated, consuming front-end resources. Additionally, reading AH when it’s not dirty can add extra latency to the operation.

Q: What is the LSD and how does it relate to partial registers

ANS: The LSD (Loop Stream Decoder) is a CPU feature that can replay short loops from a buffer, improving efficiency. Making a high-byte register like AH dirty can prevent loops from utilizing the LSD, impacting performance. Some CPU microcode updates have also been known to disable the LSD entirely due to issues related to partial register handling.

Q: Is movzx eax, al eliminated on Haswell/Skylake

ANS: No, movzx eax, al is not eliminated at rename on Haswell or Skylake. While movzx ebx, al might be, operations that write to a wider register (like EAX) from a narrower one (AL) when the destination is the same architectural register family (RAX) often still involve the renaming or merging logic.

Q: How do AMD CPUs handle partial registers compared to Intel

ANS: AMD CPUs generally do not perform partial register renaming in the same way Intel does. Operations on partial registers are typically handled by directly accessing and modifying the full register, without the complex renaming and merging logic seen in some Intel architectures.
